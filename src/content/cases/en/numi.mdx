<Section n="01" id="idea" label="The idea" title="Plan from what's already there.">
<div class="prose">

Numi flips the usual order: you scan your grocery receipt (or tell Numi what you bought), it keeps a picture of your fridge, and it plans meals around that and around your day — a late meeting means a shorter dinner.

The risk in that idea is obvious. Reading a crumpled receipt or a photo of a shelf is error-prone, and a plan built on a wrong fridge is useless. So the design principle was **visible uncertainty**: Numi shows what it read, marks what it assumed and refuses to guess when it can't see.

<Section n="04" id="process" label="Process" title="From Crazy 8s to an audited prototype." d="standard">
<div class="prose">

I started with problem framing and Crazy 8s, moved to low-fidelity flows and compared three layout variants for the Today screen before going to high fidelity. With a working prototype, I audited every AI moment against Microsoft's **Human-AI interaction guidelines (HAX)** and redesigned the screens that fell short, with clearer explanations and easier ways to correct the AI.

<Section n="05" id="tradeoffs" label="Key trade-offs" title="The tensions behind the design.">
<Decisions>
<Decision n="01" title="Refuse to read a bad receipt instead of returning a partial guess" ev="hypothesis" tradeoff="It's one more step for the user. A wrong item in the fridge silently ruins a week of plans.">
Below a confidence bar, Numi asks for another photo or method and explains why.
</Decision>
<Decision n="02" title="Label assumptions in place, not in a settings page" ev="hypothesis" tradeoff="Labels add noise to a summary screen. They're also the only way the user notices a wrong default before it shapes the plan.">
When the user doesn't mention something — like their schedule — Numi fills a sensible default and marks it “assumed” right there.
</Decision>
</Decisions>
